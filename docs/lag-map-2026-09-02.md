# Where the committed text's lag actually comes from (2026-09-02)

Status: **investigation complete, nothing implemented yet.** Eight parallel lanes
read the live path and measured this machine; five of twelve skeptic checks ran
before the session ended. Raw output: `_lag-map-raw.json` (157 KB, gitignored
candidate, delete once this file is trusted).

The job: first words now appear in about 2 s, but the text that is KEPT lands
9 to 11 s behind the microphone. This file says why, and what is worth trying.

## Seven things that turned out not to be true

Each of these is a shipped assumption the measurements contradict. They matter
more than the levers, because two of them are premises the current design rests on.

1. **A trailing pause closes nothing.** A cut point must be a speech mark that has
   *another speech mark after it* (`segmenter.py:94-98`, loop over
   `range(len(marks)-1)`). Verified by running it: 12 s of audio with one 6.5 s
   speech mark and 5.5 s of trailing silence closes **no chunk at all**. So the
   last thing said before a pause waits for the speaker to start talking again.
2. **Pauses lengthen chunks, they do not shorten them.** The policy picks the
   *latest* usable pause up to `chunk_soft_max_sec` (`segmenter.py:107-111`,
   `within_soft[-1]`). 30 s of audio with a pause every 2 s cuts at 13.7 s and
   27.7 s, not at 6 s.
3. **A decode is not flat with clip length.** `rules.py` prices the preview on the
   premise that a call costs the same whatever it is handed. True only on idle
   cores: best-of-6 rises 0.98 s → 1.47 s from 1 s to 25 s of audio, but
   CPU-seconds double (6.81 → 13.80). The encoder is flat (30 s pad); the decoder
   is linear in tokens. On a busy machine the linear half surfaces as wall time,
   the preview throttle switches itself off on long tails, and the loop reports
   "Catching up" - the exact failure the formula was written to prevent.
4. **`cpu_threads=14` is past the knee.** `transcriber.py:167` derives
   `max(4, cpu_count - 2)`. Measured on a 10 s tiny.en decode, best-of-5:
   8 threads 1.007 s, 14 threads 1.405 s. **8 is 28 % faster and uses 21 % less
   CPU.** Free, and it cannot change a decode's arithmetic result.
5. **Word confidence is expensive.** The committed decode asks for it
   (`live_session.py:267-270`), the preview does not. Cost: +0.45 to +0.6 s wall
   and +60 to +100 % CPU per tiny.en call.
6. **A commit queues behind the previous cycle's preview.** The closing chunk is
   decoded at the start of `cycle()` (`live_session.py:205`), the open-tail preview
   at the end of the previous one (`:225`), both on one worker thread. ~3 s.
7. **Two concurrent decodes on one model buy nothing.** ctranslate2 serialises at
   `num_workers=1`. Two 10 s decodes: 2.132 s sequential, 2.133 s "parallel".
   Overlapping pays only with `num_workers=2` (1.775 s) or two model instances at
   7 threads each (1.526 s).

## The machine itself

Every number above was taken on a box sitting at **37 to 60 % CPU while idle**,
dominated by two `herdr.exe` processes (`C:\Users\Shaf\AppData\Local\Programs\Herdr\bin\herdr.exe`)
with 65,700 accumulated CPU-seconds between them. Within-run comparisons are
sound; cross-run ones are not. **Before any further speed work, settle whether
herdr should be running.** It is plausibly costing more than every lever below
combined.

## The levers worth trying, in order

| # | Change | Expected | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `cpu_threads` 14 → 8, and move it into `dictation_settings.json` instead of deriving it in source | ~13 % off every decode | none to accuracy |
| 2 | Emit the committed update as soon as `_commit_closed` returns, then run the preview and emit a second update | 0 to 1.5 s per commit | low: `_commit_closed` already resets the agreement |
| 3 | Skip the preview decode entirely on a cycle where `pending_cuts()` says a chunk is ready to close | 1 to 2 s per chunk boundary | low |
| 4 | Close the open tail on a *trailing* pause, not only on the next speech mark (finding 1) | removes an unbounded wait at the end of every utterance | needs care: must not fire mid-sentence |
| 5 | Split the cycle into a commit task and a droppable preview task, with `num_workers=2` and lower `cpu_threads` | 2 to 3 s (6.82 s → 4.05 s median measured) | real: `cycle()` mutates `_ledger`, `_agreement`, `_last_stable`, `_uncertain`, `_preview_cost` with no lock |
| 6 | Run the accurate model as a background corrector a chunk or two behind the frontier, instead of only after Stop | converts committed text to small.en quality within 5-10 s instead of at Stop | CPU contention; needs 1 and 5 first |

`BatchedInferencePipeline` exists in faster-whisper 1.2.1 and is 27 to 36 % faster,
but it takes one audio input, so batching separate chunks is not native, and part
of the win is its forced internal VAD trimming silence rather than faster compute.
Not ruled out; not first.

## Why progressive commit is harder than it looks

The obvious idea - commit the LocalAgreement-2 stable prefix as it stabilises
rather than waiting for the chunk to close - hits four walls, each verified:

- The stable prefix is a bare string with no sample offset (`tail.py:41-48`), while
  `ChunkLedger.commit` demands absolute start/end samples and refuses anything not
  starting exactly at the open frontier (`ledger.py:136-151`).
- A progressively committed chunk carries `mean_confidence=None`, and the post-Stop
  polish silently skips every `None` (`ledger.py:153-164`), so the fast model would
  become the final authority on most of the report.
- The sentence-close / paragraph break only fires across a chunk that committed
  *empty* text, because chunks are otherwise contiguous and the gap is exactly zero
  (`ledger.py:100-115`).
- There is one `_forced_polish_index` slot (`live_session.py:139`), so at Stop it
  would either re-decode ~100 short chunks with small.en or none of them.

None of these is fatal. All four would have to be designed for.

## Still blocked on

**The `own` gold set has never been recorded.** `data/eval/own/` holds five
`*.script.txt` files and no audio. It is the only instrument that measures real


## How to resume

1. Read this file and `_lag-map-raw.json` (the eight lanes' full findings).
2. Re-measure the current committed lag first - `behind_sec` is already on the
   socket - so the baseline is not stale.
3. Levers 1 through 4 are independent and each is small enough to be its own
   commit on its own branch. 5 and 6 are a design job, not an edit.
4. Every change is judged by `scripts/eval/run_eval.py` against a baseline report,
   not by reading a sample. Medical-term error rate vetoes a speed win.
